\title{Layer-First Reconstruction of Separated Two-Surface Point Clouds:
Frozen Synthetic Confirmation and Building-Disjoint S3DIS Validation}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle
\thispagestyle{fancy}

\begin{abstract}
Alpha-shape surface reconstruction is controlled by a scale, but changing that
scale cannot always prevent a three-dimensional complex from connecting two
nearby surfaces that should remain separate. We test a layer-first alternative:
infer two layers from observed coordinates, fail closed when their separation or
sampling is inadequate, and triangulate each accepted layer independently in its
estimated tangent plane. A shared-quadratic trend variant handles bounded surface
bending. Parameters and acceptance gates were frozen before two confirmatory
tests. On 144 arbitrary-pose synthetic cases, the method attained mean surface
F-score 0.899, versus 0.516 for PCA-anisotropic alpha and 0.620 for weighted
power-alpha, and won every paired case; aggregate topology error was zero versus
45,606 and 11,925. On a once-opened, building-disjoint S3DIS Area-5 panel of 63
automatically eligible floor--ceiling rooms, it attained mean F-score 0.806,
versus 0.421 and 0.324, again winning every case, with zero topology error and
62/63 safe accepts. The shared-trend variant did not outperform the simpler
global-normal layer model on the real panel. The supported contribution is
therefore narrow: observed-only routing and constrained per-layer connectivity
substantially improve reconstruction for sufficiently planar, overlapping,
long-gap two-surface point clouds. Close layers, intersections, outliers,
automatic semantic extraction, and deployment remain outside the claim.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords.} Point-cloud reconstruction; alpha shape; constrained
connectivity; two-layer surface; fail-closed routing; S3DIS

\section{Introduction}

An alpha shape can represent concavity and disconnected components that a convex
hull cannot, but the reconstruction depends strongly on the scale parameter
$\alpha$ \citep{edelsbrunner1994}. A small value leaves holes; a large value fills
gaps and may fuse surfaces. Density-scaled and anisotropic alpha methods improve
this trade-off by adapting the size or shape of the empty ball
\citep{teichmann1998}. Weighted alpha complexes can additionally change the
underlying regular triangulation \citep{edelsbrunner1992}. These are important
improvements, but all still ask one complex to decide which nearby points should
be connected.

That formulation is poorly matched to a common special case: two surfaces occupy
an overlapping footprint but are known, from the observed geometry, to be
separate layers. Examples include the opposite sides of a thin gap, repeated
sheet-like structures, and the floor and ceiling of a room. Once a tetrahedron or
triangle spans the two layers, changing $\alpha$ must remove that cell without
destroying needed within-layer cells. The difficult part is then not only the
choice of scale. It is the admissible connectivity.

We study a simple layer-first principle. The input remains an unlabeled set of
three-dimensional coordinates. The method estimates a common normal frame,
separates the points into two clusters along the normal direction, measures
whether the separation is identifiable and sufficiently sampled, and accepts the
case only when frozen observed-only gates pass. It then constructs one
two-dimensional Delaunay surface per inferred layer. Cross-layer faces are not
penalized after construction; they are absent from the construction space
(Figure~\ref{fig:workflow}). A shared quadratic trend can be removed before
clustering when both layers bend together.

The contribution is empirical and deliberately bounded. First, a preregistered
144-case synthetic confirmatory panel tests arbitrary poses, six non-outlier
stresses, and strong PCA-anisotropic and weighted-alpha comparators. Second, a
separately preregistered external test uses the public Stanford Large-Scale 3D
Indoor Spaces (S3DIS) data \citep{armeni2016}. Area 5, which belongs to a building
excluded from calibration, was opened once after the extraction rule, method,
parameters, metrics, and gates had been committed. This test asks whether the
same observed-only method works on real floor--ceiling point clouds, not whether
semantic floor and ceiling instances can be found automatically.

The result is positive in that declared domain. The layer-first reconstruction
wins every paired case against both alpha comparators in both panels, while its
aggregate topology error is zero. The result also sharpens the novelty boundary:
the real-data shared-trend output is numerically identical to the simpler
global-normal ablation at reported precision. Thus the supported advance is the
combination of observed-only two-layer routing and constrained connectivity, not
a new local metric, a learned $\alpha$, or shared-trend superiority.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Observed-only layer inference}

Let $X=\{\mathbf{x}_i\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^3$ be the observed point set.
No class name, instance identity, reference point, or ground-truth layer label is
available to the method. For each point we estimate a local PCA normal from its
$k=12$ nearest neighbors. Because a normal and its negative represent the same
unoriented plane, the common orientation is obtained from the second-moment
tensor
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf{Q}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{n}_i\mathbf{n}_i^{\mathsf T},
 \qquad
 \widehat{\mathbf{n}}=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\|\mathbf{v}\|=1}
 \mathbf{v}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{v}.
 \label{eq:normal}
\end{equation}
In plain terms, $\mathbf{Q}$ finds the direction most local planes agree is
normal to the two surfaces. Each centered point is projected onto that direction,
$h_i=(\mathbf{x}_i-\bar{\mathbf{x}})^{\mathsf T}\widehat{\mathbf{n}}$, and a
deterministic two-means iteration separates the two height modes.

For bounded jointly curved surfaces, the shared-trend variant first constructs a
tangent frame $(u,v,h)$ and alternates between two residual clusters and one
quadratic height trend
\begin{equation}
 q(u,v)=\beta_0+\beta_1u+\beta_2v+\beta_3u^2+
          \beta_4uv+\beta_5v^2.
 \label{eq:trend}
\end{equation}
The two layers are assigned from $h-q(u,v)$. Both surfaces share $q$ but retain
different residual centers. This conventional mixture regression is used because
it repairs a measured synthetic curvature failure; it is not presented as a PFTF
or local-SPD contribution.

\subsection{Fail-closed routing}

Three quantities summarize whether the inferred layers are usable. The smaller
cluster fraction checks that neither layer has collapsed. The separation signal
to noise ratio is
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{SNR}=\frac{|\mu_1-\mu_0|}
 {\sqrt{n^{-1}\sum_i(r_i-\mu_{z_i})^2}},
 \label{eq:snr}
\end{equation}
where $r_i$ is either normal height or trend-removed height, $z_i$ is its inferred
layer, and $\mu_0,\mu_1$ are the layer centers. Finally, the cross-kNN fraction is
the proportion of directed $k$-nearest-neighbor relations whose endpoints receive
different inferred labels. Low cross-kNN fraction means that sampling within a
surface is locally denser than sampling across the gap.

The frozen route requires a minimum cluster fraction of 0.20, SNR of at least
3.0 in the final two confirmatory protocols, and cross-kNN fraction at most 0.05.
An unidentifiable pair is labeled unsupported. An identifiable but inadequately
sampled pair requests rescanning. A reconstruction that violates its inferred
two-component structure fails closed as an algorithm failure. Only an accepted
case is counted as a safe output. These decisions use observed coordinates and
the reconstructed mesh; true labels enter evaluation only.

\subsection{Constrained per-layer reconstruction}

For each inferred layer $\ell\in\{0,1\}$, its points are centered, projected onto
their two leading PCA directions, and triangulated by two-dimensional Delaunay.
The local triangle indices are mapped back to the original three-dimensional
points, and the two triangle sets are combined without adding a cross-layer edge
or face. The operation does not optimize $\alpha$:
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{layer}}
 =\mathcal{D}_{2}\!\left(\Pi_0 X_0\right)
  \;\cup\;
  \mathcal{D}_{2}\!\left(\Pi_1 X_1\right),
 \qquad X_0\cap X_1=\varnothing,
 \label{eq:construction}
\end{equation}
where $\Pi_\ell$ is the PCA tangent projection and $\mathcal{D}_2$ is planar
Delaunay triangulation. Equation~\eqref{eq:construction} is the central design
choice: layer inference changes which connections are possible before any scale
decision is made.

\subsection{Comparators and endpoints}

B5 is a frozen PCA-normal anisotropic alpha filtration with density
normalization. M1 is a frozen weighted regular triangulation scored by the proper
power circumradius. Both are stronger comparators than a fixed global alpha and
were frozen independently of the confirmatory endpoints.

Geometry is measured by surface F-score at a fixed distance tolerance and by a
normalized two-sided distance loss (squared Chamfer plus Hausdorff; higher
F-score and lower loss are better). Topology error aggregates component error,
Betti-number error, and true-layer bridge edges and faces. Nonmanifold edges are
also audited. A safe accept must have no component error or true-layer bridge.
Topology totals are comparable between methods within a panel, not between the
two panels, because their sampling budgets differ.

\section{Frozen experiments}

\subsection{Synthetic confirmatory panel}

The synthetic Phase-50 protocol was frozen before evaluation. It contains 144
cases: two observed point counts (160 and 256), six stresses (control, upper
occlusion, 75:25 layer imbalance, anisotropic noise, a sinusoidal common trend,
and a local bump), and 12 paired repeats. Every observed/reference pair receives
the same seed-specific proper three-dimensional rotation. Dense references use
4,096 points and evaluation samples 1,024 surface points. The gates require zero
false-safe accepts, at least 95\% overall and 90\% subgroup safe coverage, mean
F-score margins of at least 0.10, at least 75\% casewise wins against each
comparator, lower geometry loss, zero candidate topology error, and improvement
over the global-normal ablation.

\subsection{Building-disjoint S3DIS panel}

Calibration used S3DIS Areas 1, 2, 3, 4, and 6. Area 5, mapped by the distributed
data description to a separate building, remained unopened except for archive
member names and sizes until the final protocol and evaluator were committed.
The fixed extractor merges every floor instance and every ceiling instance in a
room. Eligibility requires normal disagreement no larger than $5^{\circ}$,
projected bounding-box overlap at least 0.75, at least 592 common-footprint points
per layer, gap-to-plane-residual SNR at least 10, and 95th-percentile plane
residual no larger than ten times median spacing. These annotation-derived rules
define the evaluation corpus; they are not supplied to the reconstruction.

All eligible rooms are enumerated automatically. Each layer contributes 80
coordinate-hash-selected observed points and up to 512 disjoint reference points
within the common footprint. A deterministic normalized $10^{-4}$ general-
position joggle is shared by all methods. After one opening, 63 Area-5 rooms were
eligible, exceeding the preregistered minimum of 20. The gates require no
false-safe accepts, safe coverage at least 0.90, comparator availability, mean
F-score margins at least 0.20 versus B5 and 0.30 versus M1, at least 85\%
casewise wins, lower geometry loss, and topology error at most one quarter of
each comparator. Non-inferiority to the global-normal ablation was also required.

On the synthetic panel, the layer-routed method exceeds B5 by 0.382933 mean
F-score and M1 by 0.278396. It wins 144/144 paired cases against each comparator,
and every one of the 12 point-count--stress subgroups has 12/12 safe accepts.

with no false-safe case. The one conservative rescan request, hallway 5, is
nevertheless topology-safe and has F-score 0.989640. Both alpha comparators are
available for all rooms. The candidate has zero aggregate topology error, while
B5 and M1 accumulate 5,214 and 13,375. All frozen gates pass

not validate shared-trend superiority.

\section{Discussion}

The paired improvements are large because the method changes the hypothesis
space. B5 changes the local metric of an alpha filtration and M1 changes the
regular triangulation, but both still reconstruct the union of two surfaces as
one three-dimensional complex. The layer-first method uses an observed geometric
condition---two identifiable, locally separated layers---to factor the problem
into two planar reconstructions. This structural constraint removes the entire
class of cross-layer faces rather than asking a tuned threshold to suppress each
one. The result therefore answers the original alpha-selection question in a
specific way: for this regime, a better $\alpha$ is less important than declaring
which connectivity is admissible.

The validation design limits optimistic bias. Synthetic parameters were frozen
before an untouched arbitrary-pose panel. For S3DIS, a multi-building calibration
set was used to freeze eligibility and observation rules, then Area 5 was opened
once as an automatically enumerated panel. Candidate decisions used only combined
XYZ coordinates; semantic annotations, reference points, and true layer labels
were restricted to corpus extraction and evaluation. Casewise wins in all 207
confirmatory cases show that the aggregate result is not produced by a few large
outliers. We nevertheless treat rooms, not individual points, as the unit of
evidence and do not infer broader population performance beyond this protocol.

The strongest limitation is scope, not a small numerical margin. The S3DIS test
selects nearly parallel floor--ceiling pairs with broad projected overlap and a
large gap relative to residual and spacing. Earlier wall--board calibration showed
that geometry can improve on close layers, but the current topology-safe route
rejects them because physical occlusion produces non-overlapping observed support.
The method is also not validated for intersecting surfaces, severe spatial
outliers, fewer than 160 synthetic observed points, arbitrary multi-surface scenes,
or automatic discovery of the two semantic objects. Its planar Delaunay surfaces
can bridge large holes within one layer because no boundary recovery is included.
Floating-point triangulation is used, so this is not an exact-predicate or
deployment certificate.

These boundaries suggest concrete next tests. A visibility-aware support model is
needed for close occluded layers. Boundary-aware planar meshing or a per-layer
alpha restriction should be tested for incomplete footprints. A deployment study
must add automatic pair discovery, sensor-specific artifacts, runtime and memory,
and exact or validated geometric predicates. None of those extensions is required
to interpret the present result: the current evidence already establishes a
positive, reproducible advantage in the stated long-gap two-layer domain.

\section{Conclusion}

For sufficiently separated two-surface point clouds, constraining connectivity is
more effective than continuing to tune an alpha filtration. An observed-only
fail-closed route followed by independent per-layer triangulation improved mean
F-score by 0.279--0.383 on the synthetic confirmatory panel and by 0.385--0.482 on
building-disjoint S3DIS rooms, won all 207 paired cases against both frozen alpha
comparators, and produced zero aggregate topology error. The real-data ablation
shows that this success should be credited to layer-first routing and constrained
connectivity, not to shared-trend or PFTF/local-SPD novelty. The resulting claim is
narrow, affirmative, and testable: when two approximately parallel layers are
observationally identifiable and well sampled, reconstructing them separately is
substantially more accurate and topologically safer than reconstructing their
union with anisotropic or weighted alpha methods.
